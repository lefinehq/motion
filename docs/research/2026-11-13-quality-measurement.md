# Gate, lint, then vote: measuring motion quality

No single number measures motion-graphics quality, and nobody has built one. Every established image and video metric was validated on photographs and compressed natural video. They behave worst on the flat fills, sharp edges and text that motion graphics are made of, and no human-rated dataset of motion graphics exists to fix that. Scoreboards do exist. Since mid-2026 a cluster of motion-graphics benchmarks and arenas has appeared, including HeyGen's Code2Video benchmark with a Kaggle voting arena, Design Arena's Code to Video arena, Figma-affiliated MotionGraphicsBench and Berkeley's Animation2Code. All of them hold the rendering framework fixed and rank language models, so **a public board comparing engines (motion vs HyperFrames vs Remotion) is still an open slot**. The practical stack for motion has five layers. Hard QC gates come first: safe areas, flashes, loudness, contrast and glyphs. Render-fidelity regression uses exact or near-exact pixel diffs with perceptual triage. A craft lint computed from the scene graph checks durations, easing, judder speed, text hold time and layout. Requirement checks verify each brief. AI judges serve only as a calibrated prefilter. Humans decide taste through blind pairwise votes. A credible first public board needs about **30–50 briefs, 300–600 votes per engine pair, roughly 50 vetted raters plus a separate panel of 10–15 motion designers**, Bradley–Terry scores with rater-clustered confidence intervals, and full release of briefs, agent skill packs, code, renders and votes. Much of the evidence below is from 2026 and some of it is vendor-authored. The last section lists what to re-check before quoting it.

## Pixel metrics catch regressions but cannot judge design

Rendering projects do not use perceptual metrics as their pass/fail gate. Blender's render tests compare against stored reference images with `oiiotool --fail 0.016 --failpercent 1`. A pixel fails if any channel moves more than 0.016 on a 0–1 scale, and the test fails if more than 1% of pixels fail. Every run writes a ×16 diff image, and thresholds are raised per test where noise is legitimate ([Blender render_report.py](https://sources.debian.org/src/blender/5.0.1%2Bdfsg-1/tests/python/modules/render_report.py)). Chromium's GPU pixel tests run on Skia Gold. They default to **exact matching with several approved baselines** and offer fuzzy matching only as an opt-in (pixel-count, per-pixel delta, border and Sobel-edge parameters). The documentation warns that fuzzy matching can let a broken test pass ([Chromium GPU pixel testing](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/2b15682c97f9b6f1a812d0c39044f29943b83ac1/docs/gpu/gpu_pixel_testing_with_gold.md)). A deterministic engine should copy this: hash first, a Blender-style tolerance second, human triage of the diff image third.

Perceptual metrics earn their place one step later, when ranking how bad a difference is and comparing renders that legitimately differ in anti-aliasing or font hinting. NVIDIA's **FLIP** is the only one designed for "differences between rendered images and ground truths". Its validation was a user study of whether its error maps locate visible differences, which it did "substantially better" than rival metrics. It is BSD-3 licensed, pip-installable and CUDA-capable, and the authors now recommend the **mean** as the scalar ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/flip); [NVlabs/flip](https://github.com/NVlabs/flip)). **SSIMULACRA2** has the best-documented classical score scale: 90 is "visually lossless" under a flicker test, 80 is invisible side by side, 70 is high quality. Its Spearman correlations are 0.845 on TID2013 and 0.881 on CID22. All of that validation is on photographic compression ([cloudinary/ssimulacra2](https://github.com/cloudinary/ssimulacra2)). PSNR and SSIM are cheap "did anything change" detectors and poor judges. SSIM scores roughly 0.36–0.52 SRCC on the PIPAL GAN-distortion set, depending on the protocol ([NTIRE 2021 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.03072); [PIQ docs](https://piq.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview.html)).

**VMAF** is the industry video metric and the wrong tool for comparing renderers. Netflix trained it on 34 six-second clips from its catalogue and about 300 H.264 encodes, scored by viewers under the BT.500 DSIS protocol ([Netflix VMAF blog mirror](https://segmentfault.com/a/1190000041331087/en)). It measures compression and scaling damage relative to a reference, not design. AWS documentation calls it less reliable for animation and gaming content ([AWS MediaConvert](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/mediaconvert/latest/ug/per-frame-metrics-analysis.html)). On still images it ranks poorly: Spearman 0.491 on KonFiG-IQA ([ssimulacra2 README](https://github.com/cloudinary/ssimulacra2)). Netflix's **VMAF v1 (June 2026)** adds the CAMBI banding feature and chroma sensitivity and turns on the no-enhancement-gain model by default. The write-up never mentions animation, text or synthetic content ([Netflix VMAF v1 post, mirror](https://noise.getoto.net/2026/06/20/vmaf-v1-good-is-not-good-enough/)). Use it for one job: measuring encode loss between motion's lossless frames and the delivered MP4. **CAMBI** is the only mature open metric for a specific artefact. It detects banding, which is "especially visible in animated content" and therefore the most likely visible defect in gradient- and glow-heavy motion graphics ([CAMBI, arXiv 2102.00079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00079v1); [VideoVerse ep. 20](https://buzzsprout.com/1984843/13313674-tvv-ep-20-demystifying-vmaf-with-zhi-li)).

The closest research field, screen-content quality assessment, confirms the domain problem. On the SIQAD screen-content database, PSNR, SSIM and FSIM "did not perform well" ([Gu et al.](https://kegu.netlify.com/PDF/Screen%20image%20quality%20assessment%20incorporating%20structural%20degradation%20measurement.pdf)). The natural-scene-statistics NR metric NIQE falls to about 0.48 SRCC because screen content "does not hold the statistics" it relies on ([arXiv 2008.08561](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08561)). The gaming-video model GAMIVAL had to inject "neural noise" into "extremely smooth or constant" regions before it worked ([arXiv 2305.02422](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02422)). No-reference aesthetic and quality models (DOVER, Q-Align, MUSIQ, LAION aesthetics) were all trained on photos, UGC or AI video of natural scenes. The research found **no NR model and no MOS dataset for motion graphics, kinetic typography or graphic-design video**, so their raw scores should not be trusted on motion's output. Temporal-consistency metrics have the same weakness. Optical-flow warping error is non-zero even on ground truth and rewards blur ([BlazeBVD, arXiv 2403.06243](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.06243)), and flat backgrounds make optical flow unreliable anyway.

Motion can do something no external tool can. The engine knows every text and shape mask exactly, so it can score FLIP and SSIMULACRA2 separately inside text regions with stricter thresholds. This is the cheap version of the screen-content finding that text needs separate treatment. It also knows the reference frame at every timestamp, so it can track per-frame error over time (mean, p99 and worst frame) instead of estimating flow. Pool with worst case or a high percentile, not the mean. Netflix itself flags that mean pooling hides a few bad frames ([Netflix VMAF blog mirror](https://segmentfault.com/a/1190000041331087/en)), and one overflowing caption ruins an ad.

## The scene graph turns craft conventions into exact checks

Most of what makes motion feel "professional" is convention from design systems, not experimental evidence. The conventions do agree closely enough to lint. Nielsen Norman Group puts most UI animation at **100–500 ms**, calls 400 ms "very slow" and adds that "it is far more common for animations to be too long than too short" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). Material 3 duration tokens run from 50 to 1000 ms and say duration "should increase as the area or traversal of an animation increases". Its easing tokens assign decelerating curves to entries and accelerating curves to exits ([material-components-android Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)). IBM Carbon runs from 70 ms micro-interactions to 700 ms background dims and states that "strictly linear movement appears unnatural to the human eye" ([Carbon Motion](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)). Google's design team diagnoses "stiff or robotic" transitions as symmetric or linear easing ([Google Design](https://medium.com/google-design/33089196e6c2)). These are UI conventions. Motion's target is full-frame brand video, so the bands need widening for hero moves, which the design systems themselves allow for large elements.

The experimental evidence is narrow and, in one case, contradicts a convention. Slow-in/slow-out beat constant speed for object tracking in a controlled CHI 2011 study, the first empirical support for the practice ([Dragicevic et al.](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/inria-00556177v2)). **Staggering**, a Fluent 2 default ([Fluent 2](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/motion)), had "a negligible, or even negative, impact" on tracking in a 2014 study ([Chevalier, Dragicevic & Franconeri](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01054408v1)). Stagger should therefore be checked for regularity, not rewarded as quality. Attention research caps how much can move at once. Viewers track up to about 8 objects when slow and only one when fast ([Alvarez & Franconeri 2007](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/AlvarezTracking2007.pdf)), and animations often fail because they are "too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived" ([Tversky et al. 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)). For judder, RED's starting rule is a pan no faster than **one frame width per seven seconds at 24 fps with a 180° shutter**. It notes that judder depends on how fast content crosses the screen ([RED](https://www.reddigitalcinema.com/red-101/camera-panning-speed)). For text, Netflix allows at most **20 characters per second** for adults, a **5/6 s minimum** event duration and 42 characters per line ([Netflix English timed-text guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide); [general requirements](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements)). A meta-analysis of 190 studies puts adult silent reading at 238 wpm for non-fiction ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)).

Because motion knows exact velocities, boxes and opacities at any time t, each of these becomes a deterministic check rather than a computer-vision guess. The table gives proposed defaults. Each threshold is a starting point to calibrate against designer judgments, and the basis column keeps evidence apart from convention.

| Check (computed from the scene graph) | Proposed default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Duration band | flag UI-scale moves under ~70–100 ms or over ~500 ms; allow up to ~1000 ms for full-frame moves | Convention (NN/g, Carbon, Material) |
| Duration grows with distance or area | monotonic within a scene | Convention (Material, Carbon, Fluent) |
| Easing class | no linear position/scale easing (rotation exempt); decelerate on entry, accelerate on exit; exit not longer than entry | Convention, with tracking evidence for slow-in/slow-out |
| Velocity continuity | no velocity jump at chained keyframes | Convention plus camera-path smoothness work ([Reach & North](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.09358)) |
| Judder speed for large, high-contrast areas | ≤ ~0.14 frame widths/s at 24 fps without motion blur, scaled with frame rate | Practitioner rule (RED); the frame-rate scaling is an extrapolation |
| Text hold once legible | ≥ max(0.83 s, characters ÷ 17–20 cps) | Subtitle standard and reading research |
| Independent movers at once | ≤ ~4 salient elements at moderate speed; a group moving together counts as one | Tracking evidence |
| Layout at rest frames | no overlap between non-background elements; flag 1–8 px "almost aligned" edges; text inside title-safe | Layout-metric conventions ([LayoutGAN++](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00871)); broadcast safe areas |
| Contrast against the actual composited background | ≥ 4.5:1 body text, 3:1 large text | Normative ([WCAG 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)) |
| Flashes | ≤ 3 general or red flashes per second, with the area limit | Normative ([WCAG 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)) |

Run the layout checks only at rest frames, when velocities are near zero, because that is when viewers judge composition and mid-transition frames would produce false positives. Trajectory smoothness can also be scored with motor-control metrics such as SPARC and log dimensionless jerk ([Melendez-Calderon et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2020.558771/pdf)). Nobody has validated them against perceived quality of screen animation, so treat them as diagnostics, not scores. The architectural precedent is **MoVer** (SIGGRAPH 2025). It expresses checks as predicates over computed trajectories and feeds the failed predicates back to the LLM that wrote the animation. That loop raised correct outputs from **58.8% to 93.6%** within 50 iterations on a synthetic prompt set ([arXiv 2502.13372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13372)). MoVer checks intent ("did X end left of Y"), not craft, but craft rules fit the same loop. Motion's lint is therefore an agent feedback tool as much as a score. One gap is unsolved. No public dataset of human ratings of motion-design craft exists, and no study has validated any learned model of motion-graphics quality against experts. The 660K-file Lottie corpus exported from desktop animation tools ([LottieGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11792)) could supply empirical distributions of professional durations and easing. That is a research idea; nobody has published such statistics.

## Industry separates hard QC and compliance from taste

Studios and streamers treat technical acceptability as machine-checkable and separate from creative judgment. Netflix runs automated QC, then manual QC ([Netflix QC](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000353211-Introduction-to-Netflix-Quality-Control-QC)). It requires photosensitive-epilepsy testing for **all animated content**, using Harding, Tektronix or any algorithm compliant with ITU-R BT.1702, and results must be submitted whether they pass or fail ([Netflix spec v5.1](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/7262346654995); [PSE submission](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048850953-Photosensitive-Epilepsy-Test-Results-Submission-Instructions)). Safe areas are 93% action and 90% title under SMPTE ST 2046-1 ([PBS Distribution spec](https://pbsdistribution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/PBSd-GLOBAL-TECHNICAL-SPECIFICATIONS-September-2024.pdf)). The EBU acknowledges a competing 3.5%/5% margin convention ([EBU R95](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r095.pdf)), and moving text may start outside safe action if it is readable inside it at some point ([Netflix safe-area practice](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/4406208331923-Title-Safe-and-Safe-Action-Best-Practices)). Loudness targets are −23 LUFS ±0.5 LU with −1 dBTP for EBU R128 and −24 LKFS for ATSC A/85 ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/loudness-normalization-ebu-r128-bs1770-atsc-a85)), while consumer streaming clusters near −14 LUFS ([Dolby.io](https://docs.dolby.io/media-apis/docs/loudness)). None of this needs audience data, and all of it fits in motion's CI.

Advertising splits the same way. Rule-based scorers are simple: CreativeX's Creative Quality Score is literally best practices met divided by rules tracked, optionally weighted ([CreativeX support](https://support.creativex.com/hc/en-us/articles/30535960701851-What-is-the-Creative-Quality-Rate-and-why-is-my-score-different)). Vidmob scores platform best practices and admits some "are subjective and aren't able to be efficiently measured" ([Vidmob FAQ](https://help.vidmob.com/en/articles/6314118-creative-scoring-frequently-asked-questions)). TikTok asks for a hook in the first 6 s, 9:16, at least 720p and content inside the UI safe zone ([TikTok Ads help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en)). Motion can rebuild such checks exactly from scene JSON (brand on screen within N seconds, words per card, captions present, CTA timing). It should report the result as a **compliance rate, never blended with quality**. Even CreativeX calls quality "not the full picture" ([CreativeX glossary](https://webflow-1.creativex.com/creativex-glossary)). The predictive scorers, Kantar Link AI and System1, rest on proprietary survey panels and outcome databases. Their validation is vendor-run: Kantar claims over 80% "percentile matching" on a holdout of more than 10,000 ads ([Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/inspiration/agile-market-research/can-ai-really-decode-creative-effectiveness)), and System1 surveys 150 respondents per ad ([System1 methodology](https://system1group.com/methodology)). Motion cannot copy those models. It can borrow band agreement ("same tercile as humans") as an honest accuracy measure for its own judge. Performance metrics do not work for a public board. Hook rate has inconsistent denominators and 2 s vs 3 s thresholds ([Coinis](https://coinis.com/glossary/hook-rate)), and ad position had the largest effect on completion in a UMass/Akamai study ([Akamai/UMass](https://www.akamai.com/site/en/documents/research-paper/understanding-the-effectiveness-of-video-ads-a-measurement-study-technical-publication.pdf)). They mostly measure distribution, so they belong in opt-in user case studies only.

AI-video companies reached the same conclusion about taste. Meta's Movie Gen team judged FVD and IS unreliable and ran blind A/B human studies, reporting **net win rate** (win% − loss%) on a published 1,003-prompt set, split into alignment, visual quality and realism axes ([Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/movie-gen-media-foundation-models-generative-ai-video/); [Encord](https://encord.com/blog/movie-gen-bench-explained)). Design teams use per-heuristic panel rubrics, such as 0–4 per heuristic averaged into a score ([athenahealth](https://medium.com/athenahealth-design/establishing-a-design-quality-metric-to-build-design-credibility-61236b8a4567)). Professional designers disagree heavily on pairwise UI preferences: DesignPref found **Krippendorff's α = 0.25** across 20 designers ([arXiv 2511.20513](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.20513v1)). That makes single-expert verdicts noise and multi-rater pairwise designs a necessity.

## Motion-graphics scoreboards arrived in 2026, but all rank models, not engines

The scoreboard landscape changed in the second half of 2026. One researcher found no motion-graphics arena, but the dedicated leaderboard search found several. All of them follow the shape motion uses, an LLM writing code for a deterministic renderer, and none varies the renderer.

| Board | What it ranks | Scoring | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| HeyGen Code2Video benchmark (Sept 2026) | 16 LLMs writing HyperFrames code for 168 product-launch "beats" | Proprietary pairwise judge, five axes (Engagement, Prompt-Intent, Composition, Temporal, Craft), Elo | Vendor-run; judge unpublished; human references beat nearly every model ([HeyGen Research](https://www.heygen.com/research/introducing-code2video-benchmark); [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/benchmarks/heygen/code2video)) |
| Kaggle Code2Video Arena (Nov 2026) | Same task, human A/B votes | Live vote leaderboard | Built with Kaggle and Google DeepMind ([Kaggle Arenas](https://www.kaggle.com/arenas); [Creators Toolbox](https://creatorstoolbox.com/@scout/post/n_1ez7lwvmk48n4)) |
| Design Arena Code to Video (Oct 2026) | Models directing 15–45 s HTML/CSS/JS films with Web Audio | Bradley–Terry tournaments | Leaderboard "coming soon" at launch; standings not confirmed ([Design Arena](https://www.designarena.ai/about); [RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/designarena-code-to-video-arena)) |
| MotionGraphicsBench (Nov 2026) | 32 LLMs animating supplied SVGs from 100 Lottie-derived tasks | Deterministic per-task verification programs, pass@k | 93.3% agreement with humans vs 83.3% for a VLM judge ([arXiv 2611.10592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10592)) |
| Animation2Code (June 2026) | Video-to-code reconstruction of 1,069 CodePen animations | DreamSim appearance + CoTracker motion similarity | Metrics predict human preference at ~81–84% ([arXiv 2606.28593](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.28593v1)) |
| ManimBench | Models writing Manim code | Automated checks plus cost, runtime and tokens | Methodology page timed out ([ManimBench FAQ](https://manimbench.pages.dev/faq.html)) |

The only engine-vs-engine comparison found is an informal blog test. iart.ai ran 32 builds across 8 briefs with Claude Opus 5.5 and each framework's official agent skill, using a blind Gemini judge that had to agree in both A/B orders. HyperFrames won 6 of 8 with 2 draws, and the author attributed the result mainly to the **design guidance in the HyperFrames skill, not the engine**. Remotion finished first versions faster (25.9 vs 39.7 minutes) ([iart.ai](https://www.iart.ai/blog/hyperframes-vs-remotion)). Another write-up found HyperFrames faster on render time, a different measure ([gennaroautomates](https://gennaroautomates.com/guides/hyperframes-vs-remotion/)). The lesson for motion is that a cross-engine board measures engine plus skill pack unless it controls for guidance.

Two findings repeat across every motion benchmark. First, **motion is the weak axis**. Code2Video's most common failures are timing and text collisions, and models "shrink content to stay safe". Animation2Code models get layout right and motion wrong. Graphic-Design-Bench reports models struggling with "temporal decomposition of animations" ([arXiv 2604.04192](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04192)). A board should therefore publish a separate temporal axis rather than one blended score. Second, **AI judges are useful but subordinate**. General VLM judges reach about 75% agreement with humans on motion graphics, compared with 82% for HeyGen's trained judge, and that judge reaches 98.8% on its high-confidence pairs. Programmatic verifiers reach 93.3% ([HeyGen](https://www.heygen.com/research/introducing-code2video-benchmark); [arXiv 2611.10592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10592)). General VLMs are weak absolute video scorers, with PLCC around 18–26 against 60 for the trained VideoScore2 ([arXiv 2509.22799](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22799v1)). They "cannot distinguish small details" in layouts ([Haraguchi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08885v1)), and they show position, verbosity and self-preference biases ([MLLM-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04788); [arXiv 2604.11589](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11589)). The working pattern is pairwise judging with order swap, a win only if both orders agree, per-axis rubrics, confidence gating, a judge from a different model family than the generator, and native video at 24 fps or more where supported, because frame strips lose easing.

Mature human-vote boards have converged on mechanics motion can copy. Artificial Analysis now counts votes only from validated private evaluators, requires at least 8 s of watching, makes about **1 in 5 matchups a gold check** and discards whole failed sessions. It fits Bradley–Terry hourly against an anchor model, keeps separate pools for audio and silent output, and retires prompts that stop separating models ([AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/video/methodology)). Design Arena runs four-entrant tournaments that extract five votes per prompt, hides entrants with fewer than 50 comparisons and marks them "preliminary" until about 200, and publishes system prompts ([Design Arena](https://www.designarena.ai/about)). LMArena dropped online Elo for batch Bradley–Terry because Elo depends on match order ([LMSYS blog](https://lmsys.org/blog/2023-12-07-leaderboard)).

## Pairwise votes from about 50 vetted raters beat large rating panels

For a handful of entrants with subtle differences, forced-choice pairwise comparison is the most accurate and most time-efficient subjective method. Mantiuk et al. found it "results in the smallest measurement variance". In a 30-minute session observers complete 170 pairwise judgments against 65 single-stimulus ratings when two conditions are compared, and simpler tasks gave more coherent results ([Mantiuk et al. 2012](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rkm38/pdfs/mantiuk12cfms.pdf)). Absolute ratings (ACR/MOS) are still needed to say whether output is "good" at all. ITU-T P.910 (2023) reportedly asks for **at least 24 valid subjects per stimulus in a lab and 35 in a crowd** ([Forasoft summary](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-quality/articles-vqm/crowdsourced-subjective-testing)), so a small ACR arm can run alongside the pairwise votes. Crowd screening follows the P.808 template: a qualification job, then roughly one gold item and one trap item per ten trials ([Naderi & Cutler](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08138)). Pairwise results should be scaled with batch Bradley–Terry or Thurstone. In the Thurstone JOD unit, **1 JOD means 75% of viewers prefer one option** and 2 JOD means about 91%. Ties should be avoided because they compress differences, and fewer than about 20 observers gives very wide intervals ([Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.03686)).

The research team's own binomial calculations, a one-sided exact test at α = 0.05, set the vote budgets. To detect a 65:35 preference with about 90% power takes roughly 100 votes per engine pair. A 60:40 preference needs about 200 votes for 86% power. Near chance, the 95% interval is ±9.8 points at 100 trials, ±6.9 at 200 and ±4.9 at 400. Three engines make three pairs, so **30–50 briefs with 300–600 votes per pair (about 1,000–2,000 votes in total)** give a stable first board. At 30–40 judgments per 15-minute session, that is roughly 50 crowd raters. Watch time per pair is longer for video than for images, so check the rate in a pilot. The research found no primary source on per-judgment crowd cost. Active sampling only pays once the board has dozens of entrants (models × engines × versions). Then Arena-style variance-reduction sampling or ASAP cuts comparisons substantially. ASAP reached 0.15 JOD accuracy for 20 conditions in about 40 minutes against 120 for the prior method ([ASAP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.05691); [Chatbot Arena paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132)).

The "original vs re-creation" test is a different question and needs its own design. Pérez-Ortiz and Mantiuk separate "can you tell them apart" (JND) from "which is better" (JOD) ([Pérez-Ortiz & Mantiuk](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.03686)). Show the professional clip and motion's re-creation in randomized order and side, and ask which is the original, with no "can't tell" option. A "which is better" question can follow. Claiming indistinguishability requires an **equivalence test**: pre-register a margin such as 60% detection and show that the upper 95% bound falls below it. Pooled across 20–30 clips, 10–15 designers (about ±6 points) and 60–100 crowd raters (about ±2.5 points pooled) give a solid headline. Per-clip claims need 150–300 trials per clip. Each rater should see each pair once, because repeats teach the tell. Designers and crowd should be reported as separate cohorts. Lay viewers pick the better of two designs nearly as well as experts (76.5% vs 81.2% in one website study), and the size of the quality gap matters most ([Experts vs novices](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5f6051c59e795ebda8782333/i-know-it-when-i-see-it-how-experts-and-novices-recognize)). Crowd–expert agreement in Arena is 72.8–83.1%, against 79.4–89.8% between experts ([Chatbot Arena paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132)). Expect the cohorts to agree on large gaps and diverge on easing and kerning, which is exactly where a designer panel earns its cost.

Online controls stand in for the lab. Play both clips side by side in sync, or sequentially with counterbalanced order. Require a minimum viewport of about 1280 px and full screen, preload clips completely, and drop trials that buffered. Cap sessions at 30 minutes with warm-up trials discarded ([Mantiuk et al. 2012](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rkm38/pdfs/mantiuk12cfms.pdf)). Encode every engine's output identically and strip watermarks, so raters judge design and not codec or brand tells. YouTube calibrated its crowd by having every rater score the same two anchor videos ([Yim et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12275)), a cheap trick a public board can reuse.

## A five-layer stack and a board competitors can rerun

The recommended stack automates everything except taste and uses humans to calibrate the automation.

| Layer | Purpose | Automated or human | Concrete method and starting thresholds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. QC gates | Reject unshippable renders before judging | Automated, scene graph then pixels | Text inside 90% title-safe and per-platform UI zones; BT.1702/WCAG flash check; BS.1770 loudness against a declared profile; WCAG contrast; no missing glyphs; format (resolution, fps, duration, codec, alpha) |
| 1. Fidelity regression | Catch engine regressions | Automated, human triage of diffs | Exact hash → Blender-style 0.016/1% tolerance → FLIP mean and p99 map, with ~0.01–0.05 sent to review (to calibrate) → SSIMULACRA2 ≥ 90 per frame on delivery encodes → VMAF v1 and CAMBI on the MP4 only |
| 2. Craft lint | Measure timing, easing, judder, text hold, layout | Automated, from scene graph | Table above; reported as a rate of rules passed, never blended with quality; doubles as agent feedback |
| 3. Requirement checks | Did the clip do what the brief asked | Automated predicates | Per-brief verification programs over rendered trajectories; pass@1 and pass@k |
| 4. AI judge | Cheap prefilter and tie-breaker | Automated, human-calibrated | Pairwise, order-swapped, cross-family, per-axis; calibrated on 50–200 human-rated clips; SRCC and band agreement published |
| 5. Human verdict | Taste and ranking | Humans | Blind pairwise, vetted crowd plus designer panel, Bradley–Terry with clustered CIs; separate discrimination test against professional originals |

Two design points matter more than any threshold. **Cross-engine checks must run on rendered pixels and extracted trajectories, not on motion's JSON.** Competitors do not expose a scene graph, and scoring only what motion can see would be an unfair advantage. Second, a board run by motion carries exactly the credibility problem of Code2Video, a vendor grading its own framework with a closed judge. The fix is reproducibility. Fix the agent harness and model per comparison so that only the engine and its official skill vary. Version and publish each skill pack, and add an "equalised guidance" condition, because the iart.ai result was driven by skill guidance. Pre-register briefs and engine versions. Publish every submitted variant with no silent best-of-N, sample entrants equally, and invite competitors to submit their own best skill packs. Release briefs, code, renders, agent traces and anonymised raw votes. Report cost in tokens, agent steps, wall-clock time and render time beside quality, as ManimBench and iart.ai do. Fit batch Bradley–Terry with **rater-clustered bootstrap intervals** (at least 1,000 resamples). Artificial Analysis itself warns that treating votes as independent makes intervals too narrow ([AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/video)). Declare a rank difference only when intervals separate, and hide entrants under about 50 comparisons. Keep separate pools for 16:9 and 9:16, and for audio and silent output. Stratify briefs by use case (product promo, logo sting, kinetic type, data hit, social short) and publish per-axis scores for brief alignment, composition, motion and timing, and message clarity. A second board, "which model drives motion best" with the engine fixed, comes free from the same harness. Deterministic rendering lets motion promise what generative-video arenas cannot: anyone can rerun a scoreboard entry bit for bit.

Proposed features should carry generic names, such as "QC gate", "craft lint", "fidelity check", "blind pairwise board" and "discrimination test", rather than names borrowed from third-party products or metrics. The tools in this report are cited only as references.

## Conclusion

Motion's structural edge lies less in rendering and more in measurement. Every competitor that renders in a browser has to infer layout, timing and text regions from pixels. Motion knows them exactly, so it can compute craft lint, region-aware fidelity and requirement checks cheaply and feed the failures straight back to the agent, the loop that moved MoVer from 59% to 94% correct. Every 2026 benchmark agrees that timing and easing are where AI-authored motion graphics fail. An engine that makes good temporal craft the default, and proves it with a published temporal score, is competing on the axis that is still open.

The open scoreboard slot is real but easy to waste. A motion-run board that ranks motion first will read as a mirror of HeyGen's vendor benchmark unless it is reproducible enough for HyperFrames or Remotion users to rerun and dispute it. The highest-value first step is not more metrics. It is a small human calibration set of a few hundred pairwise votes on 30–50 public briefs, together with the designer discrimination test. That set turns every automated layer above it from plausible into validated, and it is the only asset in this stack that competitors cannot copy from a paper.
